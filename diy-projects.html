Simple DIY Projects

By the Toolbelt & Trowel Editorial Team · Last reviewed: October 2026

Satisfying projects within a beginner's reach: build a sturdy shelf, plan grab bars for safe professional fitting, and freshen a weathered porch — each at a comfortable, unhurried pace.

On this page

  1. Build a shelf
  2. Planning grab bars
  3. Freshen a porch

Safety first: Every project here is simple, but tools still demand respect. Wear eye protection when cutting or drilling, keep fingers clear of blades, unplug power tools when changing bits, and use a proper step stool rather than a chair. Work at a comfortable pace and ask for a hand with anything heavy or awkward.

Build a simple wall shelf

Difficulty: EasyTime: 1–2 hours

A sturdy shelf is the perfect first project: useful, forgiving, and genuinely satisfying to make. With two brackets and a board, you can have one up in an afternoon.

What you'll need

  • A shelf board cut to length (the lumberyard will cut it for you)
  • Two shelf brackets rated for the weight
  • A drill, screws, and wall anchors suited to your wall
  • A tape measure, a pencil, and a spirit level
  • A stud finder, if you have one
  1. Decide the height and mark it. Use the spirit level to draw a faint, truly level line where the shelf will sit.
  2. Find the wall studs with a stud finder if you can — screwing into a stud gives the strongest hold. Otherwise, use proper wall anchors.
  3. Hold a bracket to the line, mark the screw holes, and fix it to the wall. Repeat for the second bracket, checking it is level with the first.
  4. Rest the board on the brackets and screw it down from underneath.
  5. Test with a little weight before you trust it with your good china.

Tip: A shelf is only as strong as what holds it to the wall. In plain drywall, use anchors rated well above the weight you plan to put up, and screw into a stud whenever you can.

Planning grab bars (and why to have them fitted)

Difficulty: Planning onlyTime: A conversation

A well-placed grab bar in the bathroom is one of the single best safety improvements a home can have, preventing falls where they most often happen. But we want to be clear and careful here: a grab bar is only safe if it is anchored into the structure of the wall, strongly enough to hold a person's full weight in a fall. A bar that pulls out of the wall is worse than no bar at all.

Safety first: Because a grab bar must bear real weight, and because getting that anchoring right depends on what is behind your particular wall, we strongly recommend having grab bars installed by a professional — a handyman, a carpenter, or an occupational therapist's recommended fitter. This is one job where paying for proper installation is unquestionably worth it.

What you can do confidently is the planning, which helps the installer do a better job:

  1. Note where a steadying hand is most wanted — beside the toilet, at the shower entrance, and along the tub.
  2. Think about the height and angle that would suit the person using it; an occupational therapist can advise, often through a free assessment.
  3. Choose sturdy, properly rated grab bars (not towel rails, which are not built to hold weight).
  4. Have them fitted by a professional, anchored into studs or with heavy-duty fixings suited to the wall.

It is also worth asking your doctor's office or local Area Agency on Aging about home-safety assessments; many areas offer help, sometimes free, with planning and even installing safety fixtures.

Measuring, marking, and the golden rule

Before any cutting or fixing, a word on the skill that underpins all good work: measuring. The old carpenter's rule — “measure twice, cut once” — has saved more wood, money, and frustration than any other piece of advice. A cut can always be made shorter, never longer, so a moment's care with the tape prevents a world of waste.

  1. Measure twice, from a clear starting point, and write the number down rather than trusting memory.
  2. Mark your cut with a sharp pencil and a straight edge, and mark which side of the line to cut.
  3. Use a spirit level or a square to keep things true; a shelf or frame that is even slightly off will show.
  4. Account for the width of the saw blade itself, which removes a little wood as it cuts.
  5. When in doubt, cut a touch long; you can always trim a little more, but you can never add wood back on.

Tip: A combination square and a good tape measure are the two tools that most improve the quality of a beginner's work. Accurate marking is most of the battle; the cutting is the easy part once the line is right.

Another easy win: a simple planter box

Difficulty: EasyTime: 2 hours

Once a shelf holds no fear, a planter box is a lovely next step — useful, forgiving, and a bridge to the growing side of this workshop. It is really just a bottomless or drainage-holed box of boards.

What you'll need

  • Rot-resistant boards (cedar is ideal) cut to size
  • Weatherproof screws and a drill
  • A square and a tape measure
  • A little landscape fabric to line it
  1. Decide on a size that suits your space and what you will grow; a box a foot deep suits most vegetables and flowers.
  2. Cut the boards (or have them cut) and screw the four sides together into a box.
  3. Add a simple base of slats with gaps, or leave it open to sit on soil, and drill drainage holes if it has a solid floor.
  4. Line the inside with landscape fabric to hold the soil while letting water drain.
  5. Fill with potting mix and plant it up, following the Container Gardening guide.

Finishing and caring for what you build

A finish is more than good looks — it protects your work and makes it last. Bare wood left unprotected weathers, stains, and eventually rots, so a coat of paint, stain, or oil is well worth the extra hour, especially on anything that lives outdoors.

  1. Sand the surface smooth, working with the grain, and wipe away the dust before you finish.
  2. For indoor projects, a clear varnish or a wax shows off the wood; paint gives color and hides lesser lumber.
  3. For outdoor projects, use an exterior paint, stain, or oil rated for weather, and give it a second coat.
  4. Let each coat dry fully as the label directs before use or recoating — patience here pays in durability.

Once finished, your projects ask for little. An occasional wipe keeps them clean, and a fresh coat of outdoor finish every year or two keeps a porch chair or planter box sound for many seasons. A small screwdriver kept handy lets you tighten the odd loose screw before it becomes a wobble. Cared for this way, a thing you built yourself will serve you far longer than most shop-bought furniture, and give a little quiet pride every time you use it. And there is a lovely permanence to it: a sturdy shelf or planter made by hand can easily outlive its maker, carrying a small piece of your care into the homes of those who come after.

Freshen a tired porch or railing

Difficulty: Easy–MediumTime: A weekend, plus drying

A coat of paint or stain transforms a weathered porch, railing, or front door, and it is honest, visible, rewarding work.

What you'll need

  • Exterior paint or stain suited to the surface
  • A brush, a small roller, and a stir stick
  • Sandpaper or a scraper for flaking areas
  • A drop cloth and painter's tape
  • Mild cleaner and a cloth
  1. Clean the surface well and let it dry fully — paint will not stick to dirt or damp.
  2. Scrape and sand any flaking or rough spots smooth.
  3. Tape off anything you do not want painted, and lay down a drop cloth.
  4. Apply a thin, even first coat and let it dry as the label directs.
  5. Add a second coat for a durable, even finish, and remove the tape before it fully hardens.

Tip: Watch the weather. Paint and stain need dry conditions and a mild temperature to cure properly, so check the forecast and the can's label for the right range before you start.

Few projects transform a home's first impression as cheaply or as quickly as a freshly painted front door, porch, or railing. It is honest, visible work with an immediate reward, and it is forgiving of a beginner, since a coat of paint hides a multitude of small imperfections and a second coat covers the rest. Spend your care on the preparation — the cleaning, scraping, and sanding — for that is where a lasting finish is truly won, and the painting itself becomes the easy and satisfying part.

With a shelf built, a planter made, safety planned, and a porch freshened, you have the confidence to tackle plenty more. Each finished project teaches you something that makes the next one easier, and before long you will find yourself looking around the house for the next small improvement to make. That quiet, growing confidence is the real reward of doing things yourself. Make sure your kit and your safety habits are sound over at Tools & Safety, and when you are ready to grow something, the Container Gardening guide is the gentlest start there is.
